At first glance, a depth map just looks like a random black and white image, but it's actually telling the software how far everything in your scene is from the camera. Unlike a normal image that stores color information, a depth map stores distance. White represents the parts closest to the camera, black represents the parts farthest away, and gray is everything in between. Normally, [music] the software just sees your footage as one flat image and has no idea what's in front or behind anything. A depth map gives it that missing depth information, letting effects react to the scene much more naturally. [music] What can they do? Once the software knows what's close and what's far away, you can do some really cool things with it. Probably the most common use is depth of field, where you can blur just the background while

keeping your subject perfectly sharp. You can also create atmospheric effects like fog, smoke, rain, and light rays that naturally wrap around the object instead of just sitting on top of the entire image. At the end of the day, depth maps are just another compositing tool that let your effects understand the depth of your scene instead of treating everything like one flat image.

How do you use them? Using a depth map is actually pretty simple. Most effects that support them will have an option where you can select a depth map as an input. Once you assign it, the effects use that grayscale image to figure out what's closest to the camera and what's farther away. From there, it's really just a matter of adjusting settings depending on the effect that you're using. For something like camera lens blur, just set your depth map as the blur map, then tweak the settings until you get the look you're after.
